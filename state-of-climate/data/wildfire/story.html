1 · Wildfire activity over time

Wildfire activity

Yukon fire seasons can swing from quiet to extreme. Two measures help put that variability in context. Area burned shows the scale of a fire season, while fire count shows how many fires were recorded. A year can have many small fires or only a few very large ones, so the two measures tell different parts of the story.

Area burned is the main long-term indicator because it shows the scale of wildfire on the landscape. Fire count adds context, but changes in detection, reporting and mapping can affect how many fires appear in the historical record.

Annual area burned in Yukon, 1960-2025. Each bar shows the total area burned in a year. Fire seasons vary widely from year to year, but the long-term record shows a statistically significant increase of about 14,100 hectares per decade.

Number of mapped fires in Yukon, 1960-2025. Each point shows the number of fires recorded in the mapped wildfire history that year. Fire count has increased significantly over the record, but changes in detection, reporting and mapping can also affect the number recorded. For that reason, area burned remains the stronger long-term indicator.

Key takeaway

Wildfire activity has increased over the 1960-2025 record. Annual area burned increased by about 14,100 hectares per decade, and the number of mapped fires increased by about 7.3 fires per decade. Both trends are statistically significant. Area burned carries more weight in the long-term interpretation because it is less sensitive to how fires are detected and recorded.

Keep in mind: these trends describe wildfire activity, not climate alone. Weather and climate influence fire behaviour, but so do ignitions, fuels, previous burns, fire management and changes in how fires are detected and mapped.

Technical details: observed wildfire activity

Dataset

The observed-fire section uses the Government of Yukon mapped wildfire-history polygon record for 1960-2025. Annual area burned is calculated from mapped fire polygons. Fire count is retained as a secondary descriptor of wildfire activity, but area burned is the primary historical impact indicator.

Area calculation

Fire polygons are calculated in an equal-area Canadian projection and converted from square metres to hectares. Annual area burned is the sum of the mapped polygon area assigned to each fire year.

Trend calculation

Annual area burned is strongly skewed and contains a small number of exceptionally large fire years. The headline long-term trend therefore uses a Theil-Sen slope for trend magnitude and Kendall's tau for monotonic trend evidence. The significance threshold is p < 0.05.

Interpretation

A trend in mapped area burned is not equivalent to climate attribution. Ignitions, vegetation and fuels, previous fires, fire suppression, land management and changes in mapping practice can all influence the observed record.

Limitations

  • The fire-history record represents mapped fires. Completeness and mapping practice can change through time. The public trend analysis therefore begins in 1960 rather than relying on the earlier part of the archive.
  • Area burned is influenced by more than climate. Ignitions, vegetation and fuel availability, previous burns, suppression and land-management decisions can all affect the amount of land that burns in a given year.
  • The number of mapped fires is especially sensitive to detection and reporting practices. It is therefore shown as supporting context rather than treated as a pure climate indicator.
  • Annual area burned is extremely variable and strongly influenced by a small number of very large fire years. Theil-Sen and Kendall are used because they are less sensitive to those extremes than ordinary least-squares regression.
  • The broader statistical sensitivity analysis shows that the exact significance classification can depend on the trend test and its assumptions. The dashboard reports the pre-selected Theil-Sen plus Kendall result and retains that methodological sensitivity here rather than implying that every statistical approach gives an identical result.
2 · Are fire-weather conditions changing?

Historical fire weather

Fire weather describes the weather and fuel-moisture conditions that influence how a fire can spread and how intense it can become. Yukon weather stations let us examine whether these conditions have changed over time.

Data behind this section
Source Government of Yukon Wildland Fire Management (WFM) weather stations
Years 1981-2025
Coverage 18 long-record stations
Yukon result Median of station-level annual values

Wildland Fire Management (WFM), within the Government of Yukon, operates the fire-weather stations used here. The Fire Weather Index (FWI) is a numeric rating of potential fire intensity. Canada's FWI System uses temperature, relative humidity, wind and rain to track fuel-moisture and fire-spread conditions. Higher FWI means more severe fire weather. FWI describes fire-weather conditions; it does not tell us how many fires will start or how much land will burn.

Locally extreme fire-weather days, 1981-2025. For each weather station, this is the percentage of valid days in a year when FWI exceeded that station's own 1981-2010 95th-percentile threshold. The line shows the median of those station percentages across the long-record network for each year. No statistically significant long-term trend was detected (p = 0.082).

Very high daily fire weather, 1981-2025. For each weather station, the annual 95th percentile describes the high end of daily FWI values reached that year. The line shows the median of those station values across the long-record network. No statistically significant long-term trend was detected (p = 0.127).

Sustained fire weather, 1981-2025. At each weather station, a 90-day running mean smooths daily FWI over roughly three months; the highest 90-day mean reached in each year is retained. The line shows the median of those annual values across the long-record station network. This measure has increased by about 0.72 FWI units per decade, a statistically significant trend (p = 0.023).

Key takeaway

Sustained fire weather is the only one of these three historical indicators with a statistically significant long-term increase. It rose by about 0.72 FWI units per decade from 1981 to 2025. The other two indicators vary considerably from year to year but do not show statistically significant long-term trends.

Keep in mind: the Yukon summary is based on 18 long-record weather stations rather than observations everywhere in the territory. Local trends can differ from the Yukon-wide median.

Technical details: historical fire weather

Primary observational dataset

The historical trend assessment uses daily Fire Weather Index observations from Government of Yukon Wildland Fire Management (WFM) weather stations. These direct station observations are the historical reference used for the indicators in this report. The two grid-based products described below were evaluated as possible alternatives but were not selected for the historical trend indicators.

The analysis period shown in the story is 1981-2025. A fixed long-record panel is used to reduce artificial changes caused by stations entering or leaving the observing network.

Station-year screening

A station-year must contain at least 120 valid daily FWI observations before annual indicators are calculated. A station must contain at least 30 usable years to enter the fixed panel.

The resulting panel contains 18 stations. Depending on individual station coverage, between 8 and 18 panel stations contribute to a given year's Yukon median.

Three annual indicators

FWI95d is the percentage of valid days above each station's own 1981-2010 FWI 95th percentile. It describes the frequency of locally extreme fire-weather days.

FWI95 is the annual 95th percentile of daily FWI. It describes the upper tail of daily fire-weather conditions in each year.

FWI90d is the annual maximum of a 90-day running mean FWI. At least 45 observations are required within the 90-day calendar window. It describes sustained multi-month fire-weather conditions.

Yukon aggregation

The annual Yukon value is the median across the fixed station panel. This prevents long stations from receiving more weight simply because they contain more daily observations.

Trend and significance

Trend magnitude is estimated with the Theil-Sen slope. Monotonic trend evidence uses Kendall's tau, with p < 0.05 used as the statistical significance threshold.

The significance labels in the main figure apply to the Yukon-wide annual median series. They do not imply that every station has the same trend or that every individual station trend is statistically significant.

Limitations

  • Weather stations are point observations. The 18 long-record stations provide direct measurements where they operate, but they are not an evenly spaced sample of the entire Yukon landscape.
  • Individual station records contain different periods of coverage. The fixed-panel approach reduces changes caused by stations entering or leaving the network, but not every station contributes in every year.
  • The Yukon-wide value is the annual median across the available fixed-panel stations. This provides a stable territory-level summary, but it can hide important differences among communities and regions. Station-level information is therefore retained in the coverage table.
  • The Fire Weather Index describes meteorological conditions associated with fire behaviour. It does not directly measure wildfire occurrence, fire size, ecological burn severity or smoke exposure.
  • FWI95d uses each station's own 1981-2010 threshold. That is useful for identifying unusually high conditions relative to the local climate, but it means the threshold itself is not the same absolute FWI value at every station.
  • Statistical significance in the main figure applies to the Yukon-wide annual median series. Individual stations can show different trend directions and levels of statistical evidence.
Other historical fire-weather datasets explored

ERA5-based gridded fire-weather datasets

ERA5 is a global historical weather reanalysis produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. Reanalysis combines observations with a weather model to provide a consistent estimate of past weather on a regular grid.

Two gridded fire-weather datasets were evaluated against the WFM station observations:

  • ERA5-CEMS - Fire danger indices historical data from the Copernicus Emergency Management Service
  • ERA5-FWI-SN - ESCER post-processed dataset

For the validation, the nearest 0.25° grid cell was matched to each WFM station and comparisons used common observation dates. The comparison covers 1981-2024.

What the comparison showed

Both gridded products reproduce a substantial part of the broad day-to-day variation in FWI observed at WFM stations. Median daily correlation with WFM is 0.77 for ERA5-CEMS and 0.74 for ERA5-FWI-SN.

The agreement is weaker for magnitude and extremes. Median daily FWI is lower than WFM by about 2.92 FWI units for ERA5-CEMS and 3.21 FWI units for ERA5-FWI-SN. They represent approximately 47.7% and 45.5% of station-defined top-5% FWI days, respectively. The long-term mean-FWI trends also differ from the WFM record.

Dataset decision

The agreement was not close enough to use either gridded product as the historical trend record. ERA5-CEMS and ERA5-FWI-SN are therefore not used for the historical fire-weather indicators presented in this report. WFM station observations remain the historical reference. The comparison is retained to document the datasets that were assessed and why they were not selected.

3 · How could fire weather change?

Future fire-weather conditions

Climate projections give us a different view: how the conditions that support wildfire could change as the climate warms. Here we focus on fuel dryness, high fire-weather days and the length of the active fire season.

Key takeaway

The strongest future signal is a longer active fire season. By 2071-2100, the projected active fire season is about 16 days longer under the lower-forcing scenario and about 30 days longer under the higher-forcing scenario. Average fuel dryness also increases more under higher forcing, but there is much more model spread around that change.

Keep in mind: these are climate projections of fire-weather conditions, not predictions of how many fires will occur or how much land will burn.

Technical details: future projections

Dataset

The future section uses processed CanLEAD-FWI climate-projection products. The products compare a lower-forcing Pseudo-RCP4.5 pathway with RCP8.5.

Reference and future periods

Changes are calculated relative to 1971-2000. The dashboard shows 2041-2070 and 2071-2100.

Spatial calculation

Only grid cells inside the true Yukon polygon are included. Yukon-wide values are area-weighted using latitude-based grid-cell weights. A rectangular Yukon bounding box is not used as the analysis region.

Ensemble information

The processed products contain cellwise P10, median and P90 ensemble fields. The dashboard calculates a Yukon area-weighted summary of each of those fields.

The P10-P90 whiskers therefore show climate-model ensemble spread. They are not statistical confidence intervals and are not p-values.

Indicators shown

BUI is used as the primary measure of sustained fuel dryness. The story also shows changes in the number of days with high FWI and changes in active fire-season length.

These are projections of meteorological fire-weather conditions. They are not projections of future area burned or future fire counts.

Limitations

  • These are climate projections, not forecasts. The two scenarios represent different possible future forcing pathways rather than predictions of which pathway will occur.
  • P10-P90 represents spread across the climate-model ensemble. It does not capture every source of uncertainty and should not be interpreted as a statistical confidence interval.
  • The Yukon-wide values summarize many grid cells into one area-weighted number. That is useful for the territory-wide story, but it can hide important regional differences within Yukon.
  • BUI and FWI describe weather and fuel-dryness conditions associated with wildfire potential. They do not include all of the factors that determine actual wildfire activity, including ignition, vegetation change, fuel continuity, previous fires, suppression and land management.
  • The processed CanLEAD products provide 30-year climate periods. They are intended to describe changes in average climate conditions rather than the exact conditions of a particular future year.
  • The displayed Yukon P10, median and P90 values are area-weighted summaries of the corresponding processed cellwise ensemble-percentile fields. They are not percentiles calculated from a separate Yukon-wide ensemble time series.
